    How the numbers work

    Erlang C vs Erlang A

    Erlang C assumes nobody ever hangs up, which makes it overstaff real queues. Erlang A adds customer patience: some callers abandon, which reduces the load on agents. For most voice queues Erlang A is closer to reality. Here, service level counts answered-within-target calls against all offered calls, so abandoned calls count as misses.

    Occupancy cap

    Large queues can hit their service level with agents busy 95%+ of the time. That looks efficient on paper and burns people out in practice. The cap adds agents until occupancy stays at or below your limit. Intervals where this happened are flagged.

    Shrinkage

    Net required is the number of agents actually handling work. To schedule adds in-office planned shrinkage (breaks, meetings, training, coaching) and unplanned shrinkage (sickness, no-shows), but not planned leave, because people on holiday are not on the roster: to schedule = net ÷ (1 − in-office − unplanned). Gross required adds all three, including planned leave, and is the basis for headcount and FTE: gross = net ÷ (1 − total). Shrinkage percentages are added together, the most common convention in capacity planning.

    Chat

    Chat is approximated as a queue where each agent works several chats at once, so the effective handle time per agent slot is AHT ÷ concurrency. This is a simplification: real concurrency drops as chats get more complex.

    Email and back office

    There is no live queue, so staffing is workload-based: items × handle time ÷ (interval length × max occupancy). In practice you can shift this work across the day, so treat the interval view as a guide and the daily hours as the commitment.

    FTE

    FTE = gross required hours in the week ÷ contract hours per FTE. It assumes perfectly flexible schedules, so real rosters usually need a few percent more.
